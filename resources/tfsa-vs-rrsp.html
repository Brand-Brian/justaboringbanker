Tax-Free vs Tax-Deferred: the boring answer

Every country names this differently — TFSA and RRSP in Canada, Roth IRA and traditional IRA or 401(k) in the US. The question that decides between them is the same everywhere: your tax rate now versus your tax rate later.

By Ryan Garneau — 30 years in wealth management, based in Vancouver, BC · Updated September 2026

This guide uses Canadian account names as the running example — TFSA and RRSP — because that's where I built my career. Elsewhere, swap in your local names: tax-deferred is an RRSP, a traditional IRA, or a 401(k); tax-free is a TFSA or a Roth IRA. The shape of the decision is identical; only the acronyms change. Check your own country's rules for the specifics.

How each one actually works

A tax-deferred account (RRSP in Canada; a traditional IRA or 401(k) in the US) is a tax-later account. Contributions come off this year's taxable income (that's the refund or deduction), growth is untaxed, and withdrawals are taxed as income — ideally in retirement, when your income and tax rate are lower. A tax-free account (TFSA in Canada; Roth IRA in the US) is a tax-never account. You contribute after-tax dollars, and — with a few country-specific nuances on early withdrawals, so check yours — everything after that, growth and withdrawals alike, is generally tax-free. Neither is an investment itself; both are containers you put investments inside.

The one question

Will your tax rate be higher now or in retirement? Higher now (peak earning years) → the tax-deferred account's deduction is worth more. Lower now (early career, variable income) → the tax-free account wins, and you preserve tax-deferred room for higher-earning years. Genuinely unsure → the tax-free account is the flexible default; you can't really regret it.

The order of operations

Before either: if your employer matches retirement plan contributions, capture every matched dollar first — that's an instant 50–100% return and it outranks everything else in personal finance. After the match, fill the tax-free or tax-deferred account based on the question above. Contribution room accumulates differently by country and account; your exact numbers live in your own government or plan-provider account (CRA My Account in Canada; your IRA/401(k) provider or the IRS in the US), which beats any rule of thumb.

The classic mistakes

Using a tax-free account like a chequing account — in Canada, TFSA withdrawals only re-add to your room the following January, so track it, since over-contributing draws penalties (Roth IRA rules differ in the US — check yours). Contributing to a tax-deferred account in a low-income year instead of just carrying the room forward. And parking either account in cash for decades — the container is tax-advantaged, but the growth still has to come from what's inside it.

Do this next

  • ☐ Capture every employer-matched dollar before anything else
  • ☐ Answer the one question: tax rate higher now, or in retirement?
  • ☐ Check your real contribution room with your government or plan provider
  • ☐ Automate a monthly contribution to the winner
  • ☐ Make sure the money inside is invested, not parked in cash
