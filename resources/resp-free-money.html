Children's Education

Most countries build in some kind of tax break for education savings. In Canada, the government adds 20 cents to every dollar you contribute. In the US, a 529 Plan skips the match but hands you tax-free growth instead. Either way, it's the closest thing to free money in your family's finances — and it goes unclaimed constantly.

By Ryan Garneau — 30 years in wealth management, based in Vancouver, BC · Updated September 2026

Canada: the RESP

A Registered Education Savings Plan is a container for a child's education savings. Contributions aren't tax-deductible, but the growth is tax-sheltered — and the Canada Education Savings Grant (CESG) tops up your contributions by 20%, deposited straight into the account, up to yearly and lifetime grant limits. When the money comes out for post-secondary education, the growth and grants are taxed in the student's hands, which usually means little or no tax at all. Lower-income families can qualify for additional grant money on top, and unused grant room carries forward within limits.

United States: the 529 Plan

A 529 Plan, run by individual states, doesn't match your contributions the way Canada's RESP does, but contributions grow completely tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified education expenses come out tax-free too. Some states also offer a state tax deduction or credit for contributions — check your own state's plan. Funds can generally be used for a wide range of qualified education costs, and many states let you use any state's 529 plan, not just your own — worth comparing fees and investment options before picking one.

The order still matters, wherever you are

An education-savings match or tax break is excellent — but an employer's retirement-plan match (often 50–100%) still beats it, and high-interest debt still outranks both. Slot children's education savings into your order of operations after those, alongside your own tax-free/tax-deferred decisions, not instead of them. Your own retirement stays ahead of the kids' tuition: they can borrow for school; you can't borrow for retirement.

What to hold inside it

Same logic as every container: match the contents to the timeline. A newborn's account has nearly two decades — a diversified, low-fee mix makes sense. A sixteen-year-old's account is a few years from withdrawals — that money belongs somewhere boring. Beware group/pooled "scholarship plan" salespeople who circle new parents in Canada, or high-fee advisor-sold 529 plans in the US: a self-directed account at any brokerage or state plan portal does the same job for a fraction of the cost.

If the kid doesn't go

In Canada, an RESP can stay open for decades, transfer to a sibling, or wind down — grants return to the government, contributions come back to you, and growth can often move into your RRSP if you have room. In the US, a 529 Plan can be reassigned to another family member, or even rolled into that beneficiary's Roth IRA up to lifetime limits under current rules — confirm the details before relying on it. Either way, the worst case is mild, which is exactly what you want from a plan.

Do this next

  • ☐ Canada: open a self-directed RESP at a brokerage — not a group scholarship plan
  • ☐ Canada: contribute enough to collect the full 20% grant each year
  • ☐ US: compare 529 plans across states for fees and investment options
  • ☐ Check current grant/contribution limits with your own government or plan provider
  • ☐ Match the investments to the years until school, and shift to boring holdings as withdrawal years approach
