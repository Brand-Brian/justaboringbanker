The Boring Budget

Budgeting gets sold as a diet: all restriction, plenty of guilt. It's actually a visibility problem. Understand where your money's been, then — the part budgeting usually skips — get ahead of where it's going.

By Ryan Garneau — 30 years in wealth management · Updated September 2026

Step one: know your number

Before any system works, you need one honest month of data. Pull your last 30 days of transactions and sort them into two piles: needs (housing, groceries, transport, minimum debt payments) and wants (everything fun). Don't judge it. Just measure it.

That's it for step one — on purpose. At this point you don't yet know how much you actually need to be saving, so don't guess. This step isn't about building a plan forward; it's about finally seeing where your money has been going, what's essential versus discretionary, and where the leaks are. That understanding is what step three runs on.

Step two: stop looking in the rear-view mirror

Did the results of your past 30 days shock you? They shocked me the first time I did this — I expected more left over at the end of the month than I actually had. The problem usually isn't the data. It's that looking backward doesn't account for what's coming, and the timing of your expenses is the number one killer of people's attempts at cash flow planning.

This is also where the industry-standard 50/30/20 rule falls apart. It's a rule of thumb that applies to almost no one — it doesn't know your rent, your debt, your timeline, or your goals. It makes far more sense to decide how much you're saving, why you're saving it, and which account it belongs in based on your goals and your timelines, instead of forcing a square peg into a round hole with someone else's arbitrary split.

So instead of a percentage, build a list: every known expense, from essential needs to the wants you're not willing to give up — but list them on a timing basis. Put whatever hits on or near the 1st of the month at the top, then work your way down toward whatever comes due at the end of the month. Order matters more than category here.

Step three: look forward, not backward

Now that you understand your needs and wants, and where your money has actually been going, it's time to flip the mirror around. Look specifically at the next 90 days — three months — starting with your very next pay period, which goes at the top of the page. Underneath it, list every expense that lands between this pay period and the next. Repeat that for three months.

Then look for a pattern. Are your expenses heavy at the beginning of the month, or the end? Are they lumped together at similar times, so they all have to come out of a single pay?

Or — if you're paid every two weeks while your expenses fall on static calendar days, like rent on the 1st — you'll notice your pay periods actually shift throughout the year. Say your first pay of 2027 lands on the first Thursday of January and you're paid every two weeks after that:

  • Jan 7 and 21
  • Feb 4 and 18
  • Mar 4 and 18
  • Apr 1, 15, and 29 (three pays in one month — is that a bonus, or just the calendar catching up?)
  • May 13 and 27
  • June 10 and 24

That April is an "extra" pay isn't luck or a mistake — it's just the math of biweekly pay against a 12-month calendar. Isn't it better to know that months in advance, instead of guessing and crossing your fingers that you'll make it to the next pay?

Step four: set your buffer

Once you can see your expenses lined up against your pay periods, you can figure out the one number that actually matters: the amount you need sitting in your account right after each pay period to make it smoothly to the next one. That number will move around depending on where your expenses land in the month — which is exactly why looking backward at last month's average never gave you the right answer.

Do this next

  • ☐ Pull one month of transactions and sort into needs / wants — don't judge it, just measure it
  • ☐ List your expenses on a timing basis, top of the month to bottom
  • ☐ Map your next three pay periods against what's due before each one
  • ☐ Check whether your pay periods shift through the year, and note when
  • ☐ Set your post-pay buffer for each period, not one flat number for every month
