Saving for a Home

A first home is a big enough goal that some countries build a dedicated account for it. Canada's version double-dips on tax benefits; the US doesn't have a federal equivalent, but a growing number of states offer something similar. Here's how to think about either.

By Ryan Garneau — 30 years in wealth management, based in Vancouver, BC · Updated September 2026

Canada: the FHSA

The FHSA (First Home Savings Account) double-dips: contributions are tax-deductible like an RRSP, and a qualifying withdrawal to buy your first home comes out entirely tax-free like a TFSA. No other Canadian account does both. If you qualify and a home purchase is plausibly in your future, it's generally the first account to fill after any employer match.

You open one as a Canadian resident of qualifying age who counts as a first-time buyer (broadly: you haven't lived in a home you or your spouse owned in the recent qualifying period — the CRA definition is specific, so check it). Annual and lifetime contribution limits apply, unused annual room carries forward within limits, and the account has a maximum lifespan of 15 years — after which unused funds can roll into your RRSP without using RRSP room. That rollover is the safety net: worst case, it becomes extra retirement savings. It also stacks with the Home Buyers' Plan, which lets you borrow from your RRSP for the same purchase (repayable over time) — a meaningful chunk of a down payment can come out of tax-advantaged accounts.

United States: no federal account, but state programs exist

There's no federal first-home savings account in the US. But roughly 15 states run their own first-time-homebuyer savings programs, usually offering a state tax deduction on contributions used toward a down payment or closing costs — the rules, limits, and even the account type vary widely by state, so check your own state treasury or revenue department. Absent a state program, a Roth IRA can double as a workaround: contributions can be withdrawn anytime tax- and penalty-free, and up to a lifetime limit of qualified first-time-homebuyer earnings can come out early without the usual penalty — worth confirming against current IRS rules before relying on it.

What to hold inside it

Match the investment to the timeline, wherever you are. Buying within a few years: capital preservation — high-interest savings or GICs/CDs — because a market dip the year you buy is the one risk this money can't afford. Five-plus years out: a more grown-up mix can make sense. The tax break is generous; don't wreck it with the wrong contents.
